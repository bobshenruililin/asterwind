# Three directions to test, not three art styles to mix

6 November 2026. The current game proves the route and consequences, but does not yet prove a professional, lively experience. This is an open creative reassessment. No story, runtime or art manifest changes accompany it.

## Diagnose the experience, not only the brush

The saved opening screenshots show an instruction-led discovery: a fairly static room and a labelled button. The current room code moves joints, wave shapes, light and pose states, but its dramatic meaning still arrives heavily through caption sequences. The room/film jump also changes the apparent production quality abruptly. A more expensive still behind the same sequence will improve finish without necessarily making it more playable.

The target should be: **I notice something, I try something, the world answers, and that answer makes the next choice matter.** Not more UI, more mandatory clicking, or more collectibles. Labels are a backup for legibility, not the main event.

## Option A: painterly cinematic 2.5D

**Look:** hand-painted gouache environments with physical brush texture and controlled chiaroscuro; expressive, restrained figures. Not paper-cut edges. A limited palette across classroom, machine and inner worlds.

**Motion:** a few carefully directed performance changes, cloth and hair settling, real eye lines, a light carried by the hand, layered camera depth. No endless Ken Burns zoom on a flat still.

**Play:** in a harbour proof, moving the lamp reveals a concealed route and changes what can be seen, but the consequential action remains clear and untimed. Once chosen, the person follows through through a visible hand/prop action before the line explains anything.

**Intro:** begin with the player's hand closing a sketchbook while the room empties. A sound from the corridor leads the gaze; the machine is glimpsed through a half-open door at believable scale. One clear exploratory action, such as lifting a returned bottle toward light, opens the impossible world. Keep the actual-campus geometry only when references exist.

**Best at:** human emotion, ambitious cinematic presentation, making visual consequences carry the argument.
**Risk:** identity drift and inconsistent paint across generated poses. Needs one excellent reference set and matched performance frames, not many disconnected illustrations.

## Option B: photographed miniature with real performance

**Look:** preserve the best current corridor/film finish: crafted card thickness, adult proportions, tactile wear, practical amber light. Replace all visible schematic bodies with matched performance assets.

**Motion:** replacement-animation holds with purposeful shifts, contact shadows tied to the prop, visibly bent paper, opening folds, the real weight of the bottle. Avoid arbitrary constant wobble; stillness should be deliberate.

**Play:** manipulation has a tiny physical pleasure before choice. Tilt a bottle and its miniature room changes parallax; turn it once to discover the window; break a paper seal with one generous action. The machine's visible depth, contact shadow, slot and bottle must agree.

**Intro:** show the same material transforming: the notebook's loose scrap becomes the label window; one edge lifts and the room is there. Keep text in the notebook editable and sparse, but do not require a word hunt to unlock the entire premise.

**Best at:** preserving the strongest existing assets while making them live.
**Risk:** swapping image poses without relighting or hand/prop continuity still feels like a slideshow. The current candidate demonstrates material, not enough motion.

## Option C: deliberate high-resolution pixel diorama

**Look:** one consistent pixel grid and limited palette, crisp clusters, no blurred photographic backdrops or vector UI mixed in. Detailed environment, simple iconic figures, strong readable silhouettes. A palette shift can mark late night to dawn.

**Motion:** expressive 4–8 frame actions, responsive lantern light, rain, machine coil turn and falling bottle, small anticipation and follow-through. Camera movement follows the grid.

**Play:** direct exploration of a compact room, generous object interaction and immediate reactions. No precision platforming or mandatory dexterity. A non-player character reacts to what the player does before presenting the consequential choice.

**Intro:** a small classroom corner is immediately interactive. Close the notebook, leave through the lit doorway, cross one short corridor, hear the machine dispense unexpectedly. The player learns the world by doing two obvious things, not reading a control card.

**Best at:** cohesive animation scope, readability and a genuinely game-like rhythm.
**Risk:** changing to pixel art is a complete production commitment, not a filter. It needs character sprite grids, environment tiles and camera/UI rules. Pixel style alone does not make a static script playful.

## Same scene, same test

Do not compare unrelated mood boards. Build the same **15–25 second** sequence in two directions first:

1. A quiet person holds something they have not shown.
2. The player can make one obvious, generous exploratory action.
3. A physical discovery creates tension.
4. Two untimed actions appear in the scene.
5. The selected action causes a visible reaction within about 150 ms, followed by a brief authored consequence.

Use minimal or optional captions on the first viewing, then accessible captions and semantic controls on the second. Ask:
- What did you notice first?
- What could you do, and did it feel good?
- What changed because you did it?
- Who did you care about?
- Did it feel like a game, a film, or a labelled slideshow?

Choose the direction that answers those best, not whichever isolated image looks most expensive.

## Concrete first-priority fixes independent of style

- Stop treating finding six words as the opening requirement. Test a one-action entry with the notebook as optional discovery/reflection.
- Remove labels that repeat what a working visual already says. Keep concise action wording where its absence makes the choice ambiguous.
- Give every interaction immediate physical feedback, then a meaningful consequence; do not add chores to inflate interactivity.
- Decide the machine's camera/projection once. Its door, side, bottle plane, shadows and interactive targets must agree.
- Keep camera and character framing consistent across room and film. If the inner world is stylized differently, make the change an intentional transformation the player witnesses.
- Let dialogue breathe over visible performance. Avoid long stretches where only the subtitle changes.
- Keep semantic keyboard controls, pause, captions, generous targets and reduced motion. A lively game must not require precise motion or fast reactions.

## What to generate now

One cohesive reference frame for each of the two shortlisted styles, using exactly the same camera, cast, prop and emotional beat; then one three-pose set for the winning frame. The attached Ellis paper cutout is only a material/identity test for Option B. Do not make a large cast batch until the comparison earns it.

No final art style, public release, film rewrite or deployment is selected by this note.
