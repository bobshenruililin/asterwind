# Asterwind: three scenario directions and interaction research

Design proposal, 5 November 2026. New fiction and design research, not an approved revision of the workbook or existing film.

## Status and current contract

This document adds creative possibilities without changing existing files, scenarios or the approved player experience. The current [contributor instructions](../../AGENTS.md) remain controlling: film-first staging; one consequential choice between two clear actions; no required precision dragging, extra inspection gates, repeated forward-clicking or explanatory dashboard; accessible semantic controls; optional post-choice perspectives rather than additional consequential choices. See the [working handoff](../HANDOFF.md) for source and authorization boundaries.

The extended loops below are deliberately labelled research alternatives. They are not a replacement specification for the current anthology. Use the three film-compatible reductions in this section before considering a new multi-step play mode. This proposal does not authorize implementation, merging, deployment, spending or changes to repository visibility.

All added characters, dialogue, timings, consequences and paper mechanics are fictional proposals. The original scenario family is acknowledged through the repository's source-grounded material; none of these invented consequences is a verified prediction about real people.

## Three film-compatible episodes

### The Roof We Owe Each Other: carry the work or change the promise

**Setup:** The harbour gathering is approaching. A friend has missed the participant-pack checkpoints. Another teammate states a limited offer of checking, and the host's own preparation is still unfinished. Establish the actual costs in dialogue and action before the choice; do not make the player hunt for essential facts.

**One choice, two object targets:** take the unfinished full pack into your work area, or fold the optional discussion leaf behind the essentials and propose the smaller pack. These are the only consequential player actions. Team agreement to reduced scope remains part of the authored response, not something the player can presume through a gesture.

**Consequence A:** the full pack is delivered; later, the host reaches a transition they have not rehearsed. The paper strip used for the rescue remains attached to the abandoned rehearsal plan. **Consequence B:** the agreed essentials arrive on time; the opening needs more explanation because participants did not receive the discussion material. Both events can be worthwhile. Nobody is made foolish merely to punish an option.

**Optional perspective:** a short participant or teammate scene makes the displaced cost understandable, without adding a score or mandatory panel. A separate facilitated discussion can change the friendship fact; do not implement it as another scored decision.

### The Minutes Beneath the Table: restore the voice or change the room

**Setup:** A meeting has twelve minutes left. Ben has interrupted Mina, who still has something to contribute. Ben also holds useful knowledge. Known venue-access requirements are viability conditions, never points to trade away.

**One choice, two object targets:** turn the speaking marker back toward Mina, offering an uninterrupted turn, or unfold a blank sheet into the centre for a short written round. Mina can pass or choose a supported way to share. Show that agency in performance and accessible dialogue.

**Consequence A:** the interruption is addressed immediately and the meeting retains more comparison time, while being given the floor can make Mina feel watched. **Consequence B:** another concern joins Mina's in the public record, while collecting and clarifying the notes uses more of the remaining meeting. Neither is a guarantee of a fair or wise decision.

**Optional perspective:** translucent paper preserves an unresolved concern beneath the final minute. Do not reveal private notes automatically. In another authored version, Mina's contribution confirms an existing fact rather than solving the plot. Participation must not require a decisive revelation to be worthwhile.

### The Argument That Has to Fly: rebuild the argument or work one join together

**Setup:** A student presentation is three days away. The evidence is accurate, but its connection to the recommendation is weak. The leader could rebuild the central slides, at the expense of their own preparation. Make Noor's intended meaning and present availability clear in the scene.

**One choice, two object targets:** move the unfinished argument into your editing space, or slide a model section between you and Noor. The kite's claim, evidence and explanation are visible story objects, not a required construction puzzle.

**Consequence A:** the rebuilt slides become clearer; a short rehearsal checks that Noor understands the revision, while the leader has practised less. **Consequence B:** Noor tries the next join herself; she may gain understanding without producing an elegant finished deck. Neither a beautiful slide nor a kindly approach certifies success.

**Optional perspective:** show who can explain the joined pieces. A three-hours variation belongs in the facilitated counterfactual discussion unless the creative contract is separately revised. Do not change the original three-day setup or treat taking over as inherently bad leadership.

## Creative principle

Make the paper world carry a real consequence. A loose strip can be an unaccepted request; a borrowed strip can show displaced work; a transparent layer can preserve dissent; a repaired join can show an argument understood and carried by another person. Let composition, performance and a small number of clear controls communicate these meanings. Do not make every idea a visible interface component.

## The question behind the choice: what is leadership?

An exploratory philosophical thread can join the anthology without answering the question for the player. Leadership might involve giving direction, coordinating work, creating the conditions for others to act, accepting responsibility, or changing a shared purpose. A single action can serve several of these and can also be an attempt to escape discomfort. The game cannot diagnose the player's motives from a click.

Each episode should make one question concrete:

- **The Roof We Owe Each Other:** Is responsibility doing the unfinished work, making its cost visible, renegotiating the promise, or some combination? What did I consider mine to decide, and who had to bear the result?
- **The Minutes Beneath the Table:** Does a formal opportunity to speak become a real opportunity? When does exercising authority make someone else's agency possible? Would I defend the same process if it did not change the result?
- **The Argument That Has to Fly:** Am I responsible for the thing we deliver, the capability we leave behind, or both? What can this person now do without me, and what did we give up to make that possible?

Let the player consider both the group and themselves: what they noticed first, whose account they trusted, which cost they accepted, and what would make them reconsider. These are questions about this particular decision, not evidence of a permanent leadership identity. Changing an answer under changed facts may be judgment rather than hypocrisy. Keeping it may be principled rather than stubborn; the explanation matters.

### An optional mirror moment

After a consequence, the scene can settle on a dark, gently reflective paper surface: a window, polished stage panel or folded silver-paper screen. Leave the story's traces in that surface rather than replacing the film with a blank black page. A faint empty place in the composition can invite the viewer into the question without depicting or analysing their face.

This is an art direction, not a technical promise that the user's screen will show their reflection. Some screens are matte, some rooms are bright, and some players will not see the image. Never require glare, increase brightness, assume the player sees themselves or use a webcam, face detection, microphone or biometric interpretation. No permission prompt or personal-data capture is part of this idea.

One brief line can arrive after a pause: **“What did you make possible? What did you make someone else carry?”** Offer a quiet continue action immediately; reflection is optional, untimed and unscored. Keep text legible with sufficient contrast and provide the same line in the accessible reading order. Reduced-motion mode can show the settled composition immediately. Do not use the dark surface as a hover-dependent control or make players wonder whether playback broke.

Alternatives can be equally reflective: the empty chair left after the meeting, the reverse of the plan showing an erased alternative, a repaired seam held in two hands, or the quiet sound of the room after everyone has left. Caption any essential sound. The strongest version recalls an actual choice and consequence, rather than adding a generic inspirational quote.

A player may simply think, discuss with a partner, or skip. Do not automatically record written reflections, infer a personality or display their private reasoning to the class. A future optional notes feature would need a clear storage and sharing design first. Any classroom sharing should be voluntary.

## Optional expanded-play research

The sections below deliberately explore a different interaction format from the current film contract. They are design research, not instructions to implement these extra decisions, inspection gates, resource surfaces or result ledgers in the current film. Any adoption requires creative review. The film-compatible versions above remain the recommended implementation starting point.

### Core direction

Make the paper world do the thinking. The player should fold a commitment, expose an assumption, move a burden, edit a decision procedure and live with an imperfect result. A dialogue choice followed by a beautiful animation is still a quiz. These interactions change what can happen next.

The fantasy is not “be the best leader.” It is “help this particular group do something that matters, when no one can protect everything.” Treat the students as adults with experience of deadlines, friendship, uneven effort, face, authority and voluntary work. Other characters have boundaries and can decline a proposal. The player is one participant, not an omniscient director.

### Shared grammar, different games

Each episode has a different central verb and a short common rhythm: notice → intervene → receive a response → revise → see what remains. Let actions combine and be sequenced rather than forcing four mutually exclusive identities. A proposed assignment is visibly different from an accepted commitment. Before committing, show a short plain-language account of what the gesture will do. After committing, the paper object changes for a causal reason.

Paper carries memory, not moral judgment. A crease records an earlier promise; an overlay preserves a dissenting account; a patch records a workable repair. A pristine object is not automatically better than a repaired one. Do not make tears equal failure, darkness equal bad leadership or an unfolding flower equal the correct answer.

## 1. The Roof We Owe Each Other

**Underlying case:** The Ghost Teammate / The Unfinished Pack. **Play:** allocate and renegotiate. **Length target:** 5–7 minutes, to test rather than promise.

### Situation and object

The society's harbour gathering is in forty-eight hours. Alex, your friend and cofounder, has missed two checkpoints for its participant pack. Sam covered last time and now offers only twenty minutes of checking. You could rescue the pack, at the cost of hosting preparation.

The pack is a folded-paper roof over the gathering. Its three leaves are essential practical information, arrival guidance and discussion preparation. Each leaf carries its actual task label and estimated remaining work. The participants beneath it are people, not collectible units. Removing the discussion leaf changes the opening activity rather than causing cartoon harm.

Prototype estimates are authored assumptions, not psychological measurements. Preserve the source's ninety-minute overall work estimate and Sam's twenty-minute limit. Split the estimate into task durations only after testing a consistent production model.

### Playable sequence

1. Unfold the plan. Three tasks are uncovered. Lift Alex's draft flap to see what exists. Read Sam's offer. Use a plain button to ask Alex what he can deliver at a specified checkpoint. This reveals a concrete response, not an empathy reward.
2. Build a plan on the table. Four starting tools are available: keep ownership with Alex and set an inspectable first deliverable; take over; remove the optional discussion material; request named contributions. These can be combined. Moving a task onto Sam's paper does not assign it automatically: it creates a request he may accept within his stated offer or decline.
3. Tie the plan to a review point. The player must name the first deliverable, who has accepted it, and what happens if it is not ready. The clock advances only when the player commits an action, never while reading.
4. Open the checkpoint envelope. In the first authored version Alex has a usable partial draft, but a remaining gap. In another test version he has not delivered. Both were specified before play. The player can alter the plan using actual new information; the game does not secretly punish whichever leadership style they used.
5. Open the gathering. A short scene shows the result and the burden left behind: a complete pack with an underprepared host; a smaller pack whose opening needs more explanation; or shared work with an awkward consistency issue that was caught in review. At least two different plans must produce credible, worthwhile events.

### Concrete choices with costs

- “Alex keeps the work. Show me the essential page at six; I will keep a fallback slot.” Preserves ownership; reserves real capacity elsewhere.
- “I'll finish it. Sam, check for twenty minutes only.” Buys delivery confidence; spends hosting preparation and leaves a later accountability conversation open.
- “Send the essentials. We will explain the discussion exercise on arrival.” Saves production work; reduces advance preparation and uses event time.
- “Ask Lin for the map, Alex for practical information, and Sam to check.” Works only if accepted; incurs handover and assembly work. It cannot quietly treat volunteers as spare capacity.

### What the paper means

A borrowed strip visibly connects the completed task to the other commitment it displaced. Folding optional scope out of view never deletes it from the record. A requested strip stays loose until accepted. No hidden “trust −10.” At the end the player can trace each cost back to a decision.

**Ending question:** “You kept the event intact. Whose evening changed?” Then a short replay changes only one fact: Alex is no longer your friend. Same work, same constraints, same outcome rules. Compare the evidence and standard the player used, not their moral worth.

## 2. The Minutes Beneath the Table

**Underlying case:** The Loudest Voice / Before the Vote. **Play:** redesign a conversation. **Length target:** 5–6 minutes.

### Situation and object

Two viable venues, twelve minutes left, a vote due tonight. Ben has useful experience but has interrupted Mina twice. Mina closes her notebook. Her information may or may not change the decision.

The meeting is a stack of translucent paper minutes. The top sheet contains what the room has actually heard. Underneath are members' notes, including things they have not chosen to share. Turning a page does not give the player permission to reveal private content. A character may offer a note, read it themselves, ask the chair to read it, or pass.

### Playable sequence

1. Hear a short interruption. A visual transcript allows replay or skipping audio. The player can stop it immediately or let the current statement finish. No reaction-time score.
2. Lay procedure strips into the remaining agenda: restore Mina's uninterrupted turn; hold a silent written round; offer short turns with a pass option; take a two-minute private check-in; ask a bounded factual question; summarise and vote. The strip names its estimated meeting-time cost before placement. Reading and accessibility controls cost nothing.
3. Receive different information depending on the procedure, without inventing different underlying facts. A silent round may include another member's concern. A private check-in may produce Mina's choice to submit a written note. Equal turns may repeat known material. Direct intervention may be efficient but make Mina feel watched; she can choose not to speak.
4. Assemble the public minute. Put “confirmed,” “unverified” and “preference” tabs on the actual statements. Before sealing the minute, show authors the proposed summary through authored responses. Mina can say, “That isn't what I meant.” The player must amend, record the disagreement, or deliberately proceed with it visible.
5. Decide, defer within the available real options, or make a conditional choice with an owner for the unresolved check. Known access requirements are non-negotiable viability conditions, not currency to sacrifice for a higher score.

### A meaningful branch

Mina writes, “The lift closes before our event ends.” The chair can verify it through an available information card, change the event timing if genuinely allowed, or rule that venue out. In a counterfactual version her note confirms information already known. Fair participation should still matter. Otherwise the game teaches that quieter people deserve a voice only when they carry the plot's crucial revelation.

Ben is not a villain or a silence target. An interruption can be stopped while his factual knowledge remains useful. “Speak less” is not the same as “your contribution matters less.”

### What the paper means

An approved comment becomes an overlay in the public record. An unresolved disagreement remains legible beneath the final decision. The table changes when the information flow changes. Decorative light does not certify inclusion.

**Ending:** Two people give brief, potentially conflicting accounts of the same meeting. “I knew how to contribute” and “We ran out of time to compare costs” can both be true. Ask, “Which part of your procedure made this decision more reliable, and what did it leave unresolved?” No majority-vote morality meter.

## 3. The Argument That Has to Fly

**Underlying case:** The Weak Slides / The Unfinished Slides. **Play:** co-author, hand over and check understanding. **Length target:** 6–8 minutes.

### Situation and object

The presentation is in three days and counts for 30% of the course grade. Noor's evidence is accurate, but her slides do not connect it to the recommendation. She has forty-five minutes now. You could rebuild the two central slides in an hour, which is also your transition-rehearsal hour. A secondary comparison is useful but optional.

The argument is a paper kite: claim, evidence and explanation are separate pieces joined by strings. This is a diagram of the presentation's structure, not a physics puzzle or test of dexterity. A text outline provides the identical interaction.

### Playable sequence

1. Inspect a claim, a piece of evidence and the missing explanation. The content is readable and concrete. Students do not need outside trivia to diagnose the gap.
2. Choose how the next block will work: take editorial ownership; build one section together and hand over the next; give a clear model and observable changes; or narrow the argument and cut the optional comparison. All are plausible starting moves.
3. Act, not merely select. In joint work, the player chooses a question or demonstration, then Noor responds. In takeover, the player places an explanatory link and asks Noor to check the intended meaning. In a clearer-brief route, Noor returns an independent attempt at the checkpoint. In reduced scope, the removed comparison remains beside the kite as visible lost nuance.
4. Test the handover. Noor explains the recommendation in an authored short rehearsal. The visible deck may be improved while her understanding is still partial; equally, an editorial rescue can succeed if the explanation was shared. Coaching does not guarantee success just because it is kind.
5. Decide whether to revise, practise, narrow further or accept a remaining weakness. The event goes ahead. The ending reports both the argument the audience saw and who is ready to present it.

### Distinct routes, not a disguised ideal answer

- Takeover may produce the clearest slides quickly and a successful presentation, but the leader's transitions are less rehearsed.
- Joint work may leave a less elegant deck and a teammate who can independently defend the reasoning.
- A precise brief may work very well if Noor misunderstood expectations; it may not resolve a skill gap.
- Reducing scope can produce a coherent shorter argument while losing a comparison that another team member cared about.

### What the paper means

A joint seam records who can explain the join, based on an actual rehearsal response, not a magic competence number. Removing a claim changes what the kite can carry. The player cannot make a weak argument true with a prettier fold.

**Counterfactual replay:** Change three days to three hours, keeping Noor, the evidence and the marking criteria unchanged. Ask what the player would keep, abandon or make temporary. Do not equate changing strategy with inconsistency.

## Points and feedback: use measurement only where it means something

### Three viable designs

1. **No numerical score:** scenes, a visible decision history and contrasting participant accounts. Strongest for rich discussion, weakest for players who cannot tell whether anything changed. Needs precise causal feedback, not vague poetry.
2. **Operational numbers, no total:** minutes, remaining work, accepted commitments, unresolved factual questions and optional scope retained. Recommended. These are quantities in the authored situation, with stated units and assumptions. Do not add unlike units into a leadership score or pretend a person's trust can be measured precisely.
3. **Optional process challenge:** earn inspectable marks for actions such as recording an accepted owner, identifying evidence that would change the plan, or comparing one altered fact. These can motivate replay, but are easy to game by clicking everything. If tested, call them a record of what the player tried, not proof of quality; never let checklist completion override a character's refusal or a factual constraint.

Reject a public leaderboard, a “good leader” percentage, a hidden moral score and personality diagnoses. A conditional scenario-completion goal can still exist: the essential information is delivered; the venue meets the known requirements; a presenter can explain the actual argument. A plan can satisfy its task while leaving serious costs worth discussing.

### What the player sees

A compact ledger with four plain sections: **What changed; Who accepted the work; What you set aside; What remains uncertain.** Every line links to the decision or observation that supports it. Characters' reactions are attributed to them, not presented as an objective trust measure. Conflicting accounts remain possible.

A collected paper object can be a reward for finishing an episode, with different forms for different histories but equal care and beauty. It must not make one route the shiny superior ending. Replay uncovers alternate consequences, not a gold medal.

### Source distinction

The revised workbook still specifies six style counts, tied results and a situational-fit count, softened to “suggested response for the facts given,” plus explicit non-diagnostic framing. Overview rows 43–51 and Screen Copy rows 7–10 are the relevant handoff instructions. The separate October scenario manuscript proposes abandoning style/persona tallying. The recommendation here is a new design proposal, not a claim that the original workbook already removed scores.

If the professor requires the six-style framework, put it in an optional debrief. Describe moves in this episode using the framework, allow overlap, and explain the interpretation. A firm interruption can enable participation; a rubric does not inherently mean “democratic.” Do not force every action into a single exclusive style to make a radar chart work.

## Audience, agency and medium

- Solo first, then optional pair discussion. Private reasoning before a classroom poll reduces pressure to guess what the professor wants. Never require personal disclosure or publish individual choices without consent.
- In pairs, one learner acts and the other records the cost borne by someone absent; swap roles at the checkpoint. The observer does not score the player.
- Classroom reveal: compare two anonymous decision histories that both worked but preserved different things. Ask what fact would reverse each decision. Avoid “most popular answer” as correctness.
- The professor gets a source map, authored assumptions and debrief prompts, not a personality dashboard of students. If assessment is required, evaluate the explanation separately using transparent criteria, not hidden gameplay scoring.
- Every drag, fold and rotation has a click and keyboard equivalent with the same consequences. Large readable text, visible focus, screen-reader state updates, no color-only clues, captions and optional audio description, reduced motion, no mandatory sound, no real-time countdown. Preserve agency in accessible mode; do not substitute a lesser text quiz.
- The paper illustration is a scene layer over semantic interface controls. Avoid baking essential text into images or relying on a canvas-only interface.
- On phones, use one decision surface at a time, not a tiny desktop theatre. Keep a persistent “What has happened?” recap and allow save/resume and undo before commitment.
- One authored first outcome and one controlled counterfactual are enough for the initial build. Do not spend production effort on dozens of shallow endings. Prefer deterministic branches initially; if randomness is later introduced, disclose it and hold the seed constant for comparisons.

## Prototype sequence and decision gates

1. **Physical greybox:** Build The Roof We Owe Each Other with twelve or fewer cards, loose strips and acceptance tabs. No finished art. Test whether players can describe the consequence of moving a task and whether each serious route is defensible under some stated condition.
2. **One web vertical slice:** Brief scene → inspect → propose → accept/decline → checkpoint → revise → consequence ledger → one-fact replay. Use placeholder illustration and real text. This proves agency before cinematic production.
3. **Six exploratory student playtests, not a validated study:** include people unfamiliar with the game, mobile users, and differing familiarity with leadership vocabulary. Ask them to think aloud, then identify who bore a cost and what evidence would change their action. Watch for “I'm choosing what the game wants.” This sample finds usability issues; it cannot establish educational effectiveness.
4. **Accessibility checks:** keyboard-only, screen reader, text zoom, reduced motion and narrow phone viewport. Include disabled users where feasible; an automated audit is not a substitute for their experience.
5. **Professor/content review:** verify source alignment, mature trade-offs, fair treatment of characters, the limits of the style framework and whether any course-specific claims have evidence. Do not assume approval.
6. **Build the second verb:** The Minutes Beneath the Table. If it feels like the allocation episode with new art, rewrite it. Only then add the co-authoring episode.
7. **Polish after causal clarity:** commission paper transformations for decisions players already understand; add sound and cinematics without hiding the state change.

### Playtest red flags and repairs

- Everyone identifies one obviously virtuous answer before reading the facts: revise constraints and consequences, not merely the wording.
- Players click every question for free rewards: remove collectible information rewards and make relevance, not exhaustive clicking, matter. Never penalise reading or accessibility use.
- Players believe the game measured their character: replace result language and remove unsupported precision.
- A character always complies: add genuine acceptance, refusal and renegotiation.
- Art makes the consequences hard to understand: add a plain-language state change and simplify the visual metaphor.
- Players cannot name anything their decision changed: the branch needs a real difference in information, burden, scope or next action.
