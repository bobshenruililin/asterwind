# ASTERWIND
*A paper-theatre leadership piece in one choice*

## Three Pitches

### 1. The Blank Sail
**Logline:** It's 2:47 a.m. and the presentation is due at nine. One teammate's quarter of the project is still blank, and she isn't answering.

**The image:** The group project appears as a paper ship called the *Asterwind*. Each sail is one person's section. Three sails are covered in writing. One is white and flapping.

**The choice:** Pick up the scissors and replace her sail with one you write yourself, or send her a message and wait.

**The costs:** If you rescue the project, it succeeds, but she shrinks and you never find out why. If you wait, she stays involved, but the grade drops and you lose a teammate who needed that grade.

### 2. The Folded Note
**Logline:** In a team meeting, one person's words become huge paper banners that cover the stage. The quietest member holds a better idea, folded small in his fist.

**The choice:** Say his idea out loud for him, or hold the silence and leave room for him to speak.

**The costs:** If you speak for him, the idea survives, but the group credits you and he learns he needs someone to speak for him. If you hold the silence, you sit through nine painful seconds. He might speak, or the idea might die while he knows the floor was his.

### 3. The Ledger
**Logline:** It's peer-evaluation night. A teammate contributed almost nothing, and you're the only one who knows her father is in the hospital.

**The image:** A brass scale made of paper. Everyone's effort is shown as stacked paper cranes.

**The choice:** Rate her honestly, or protect her.

**The costs:** Honesty is fair to the others but breaks her trust. Protecting her means the hardest workers quietly make up for her, and you become the keeper of a secret.

**I'm developing Pitch 1.** Scissors are the most physical verb paper theatre has, and every student in the room has felt this exact temptation at 3 a.m. It also contains the deepest lesson of the three: **rescuing people stops you from learning about them.**

## ASTERWIND: "The Night Before"
*Running time: about 3:40*

**The world:** A toy theatre built in a shoebox. The stage frame is cut from a cereal box. Stars are pinholes with light behind them. The sea is strips of lined notebook paper turned by a visible hand crank. Every character is a flat cutout on a stick, and we can see the sticks. The player's cursor is a lantern.

**Cast:**
- **You:** an ochre-coloured figure.
- **Marco:** his sail is crammed with tiny handwriting, with footnotes clinging to it like barnacles.
- **Ines:** a figure in a small side box: a window that stays dark.

### 0:00–0:30: Prologue
*Sound: a match strikes. Paper rustles like wind.*

**NARRATOR** *(warm, dry, a stagehand who has seen this a hundred times):*
"This is the *Asterwind*. Three people built her from printer paper and caffeine. She's due at nine."

The camera moves along the sails: Marco's dense one, your clean one, and the third, which is blank, white and moving gently in the draft.

### 0:30–1:30: 2:47 a.m.
The paper sea stops moving. The ship is stuck.

Group-chat messages fold themselves into paper birds and fly across the stage, landing in the rigging:

> **Marco:** has Ines sent ANYTHING
> **Marco:** this is 25% of the grade
> **Marco:** I can't lose the scholarship over this

That last bird lands on your figure's shoulder, and the paper creaks under its weight.

Ines's window stays dark. Her status reads: *last seen 9:14 p.m.* We know nothing more, and neither do you.

**NARRATOR:**
"You could write her section by dawn. You're good at this. That's sort of the problem."

Two objects are lowered on strings into the lantern's light:
- **Silver scissors.** Hovering over them makes a dotted line appear around Ines's sail.
- **A paper phone.** Hovering over it shows a drafted message: *"Hey, no judgment. What do you need to get this done?"*

There are no buttons and no prompt text. The player reaches for one of them.

---

### PATH A: The Scissors
The player cuts the sail by dragging slowly along the dotted line, with a loud, crunchy scissor sound. **Making the player do the cutting matters. They should feel complicit.**

The blank sail drifts into the notebook sea and sinks. You glue in a new sail cut from your own ochre paper.

Time-lapse: the lantern burns down and the pinhole stars wheel overhead. At 6:40 a.m. a bird arrives:

> **Marco:** you're a legend

The sea starts moving again and the ship glides. The professor, a huge paper moon, stamps the sail with a red wax **A−**.

Ines's figure slides in from the edge of the stage. She's holding something rolled up behind her back. She looks at the ochre sail.

**INES:** "Oh. You… okay. Thanks."

She puts the roll in her bag. **We never see what was on it.** As she leaves, a single crease appears across her figure, and she is now folded a little smaller.

**Coda: next semester.** A new ship, and every sail is ochre. Your figure is holding all the rigging strings, one in each hand and two in its teeth.

**NARRATOR:**
"They called it leadership. Some nights, you'll even believe them."

---

### PATH B: The Phone
The player sends the message. Then nothing happens.

**This is the core of this path: the player has to sit through the wait.** The lantern flickers and the paper clock hands creak forward. Marco's birds keep arriving:

> **Marco:** ??
> **Marco:** are you actually waiting
> **Marco:** just do it yourself

The scissors stay hanging in view, swaying slightly, still tempting. *(Director's option: the scissors stay usable. If the player gives in partway through, they get Path A with a new opening line: "You waited forty seconds. It felt like hours.")*

At 4:12 a.m. a bird arrives with flour smudged on its wings:

> **Ines:** bakery shift ran over, manager wouldn't let me leave. i have half. it's not good. i'm so sorry

Her window finally lights up with the orange glow of an oven.

At 7:30 she arrives carrying a sail patched together from receipts and a flour sack, written in pencil. Your two figures stitch it in together, sharing one needle.

The ship sails, but it leans to one side. Her section wobbles during the presentation. The paper moon stamps a **B−**.

Marco says nothing. He unpins his own sail, the one covered in barnacle-footnotes, and carries it off stage. One last bird drops behind him:

> **Marco:** I needed this one.

**Coda: next semester.** A mismatched ship with one patched sail. Ines is at the mast, holding her own rope. Far off on the horizon, Marco's sail is on someone else's ship.

**NARRATOR:**
"You kept one person. You lost another. Nobody tells you both of those are the job."

---

### Final 20 seconds (both paths)
The lantern swings behind the paper screen, and **the ending you didn't choose plays as a shadow puppet** for five seconds, with no words. If you cut, you see the shadow of Ines stitching. If you waited, you see the shadow of your figure holding every string.

Then a real, photographed human hand reaches into the shoebox and sets the scissors down on the table.

**NARRATOR:** "Nine o'clock came, the way it does."

Title card: **ASTERWIND**

## What It Actually Teaches
- **Fixing a problem yourself is not the same as dealing with it.** This is Heifetz's distinction between technical work and adaptive work. The scissors solve the deadline, but they don't solve the team.
- **Rescuing someone erases information.** In Path A you never find out about the bakery. The leader who catches every failure never learns why things are failing.
- **Every choice disappoints someone.** You aren't choosing right or wrong. You're choosing *whose* trust to spend, Ines's or Marco's, and Marco's grievance is legitimate.
- **Rescuing creates dependence.** The ochre ship shows that the person who catches everything also ends up being the limit on what the team can do.

**Questions the professor could use afterward** (in conversation, not on screen):
1. What was on the roll in Ines's bag?
2. Was Marco wrong?
3. Was there a third option you wished you'd had?

## What Makes an Audience Feel Something
1. **The player's hand does the act.** Cutting along the dotted line feels like a betrayal in a way a button click never could.
2. **Silence is the hard version.** Choosing to wait means sitting still while the scissors keep swaying in view.
3. **Small physical consequences.** One crease in Ines's figure, or Marco unpinning his sail, lands harder than any line of dialogue.
4. **Specific details.** A flour-smudged bird, a sail made of receipts and the word "scholarship" make it feel real.
5. **The unchosen ending appears as a shadow.** It shows the other cost without a replay screen and without telling anyone they were right.
